The single most important question when you buy a used part. Here's how to answer it before you spend a dollar — and how we'll answer it for you if you'd rather just call.

Start with the fitment list

Every listing on this site shows the vehicles that part interchanges with — years, makes, and models, straight out of the industry interchange database. If your vehicle is on that list, the part is a match for your application.

Interchange is why a part off a Chevrolet Tahoe can be the correct part for a GMC Yukon: the manufacturer used the same component across the platform. The listing tells you which ones.

When the list isn't enough

Some parts vary by things a year/make/model can't capture:

  • Engine size and drivetrain — 4.8L vs 5.3L, 2WD vs 4WD
  • Trim and options — heated seats, tow package, upgraded audio, sunroof
  • Build date — mid-year production changes are common
  • Casting or part numbers stamped on the component itself

The three numbers on a listing

Salvage listings carry more than one number and they do not mean the same thing. Ours are:

  • Part ID (also called the R#) — the one number that identifies this part and no other. It is on the part's tag, at the top of the product page, and on your invoice. Use this one when you call, order, or return something.
  • Vehicle stock number — identifies the car the part came off. Every part removed from that same car shares it, so it is useful for matching a set, but it will never single out one part.
  • Interchange number — identifies a family of parts that fit the same applications, such as 641-00118L. Several different parts can share one, and it is what tells you a part off another model still fits yours.

When one of these matters, the safest move is to compare the number on your old part to the number in the listing photos. Where the listing shows the part itself, the numbers are usually readable; where it shows the donor vehicle instead (the listing says so), call us and we'll read the number off the part for you.
